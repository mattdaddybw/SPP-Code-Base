## Start Here: Guided Observations

Click an observation to expand it.

### Observation 1

Poverty is the strongest factor, but it is not the whole story. Grade 3-5 reading achievement declines predictably as the percentage of students qualifying for Free or Reduced-Price Lunch increases. But poverty does not explain everything. Use the district finder tool to find Spero Academy in Brooklyn Park. Its achievement is lower than that of many schools, including many in the bottom 10%. Notice, however, that its actual and predicted rates of achievement are nearly identical. That means the school is performing about as the model would expect given its student enrollment. In particular, the school serves a very large share of students receiving special education services, which the model takes into account. You can learn more about what the model includes on the Model Explorer page. You can also learn more about a school's enrollment by using the state's online Report Card system.

### Observation 2

Most large districts do not have any schools in the top 10%. To explore this group, select “Districts with 4 or more elementary schools” in Overall View. Then choose a few familiar districts and see how many of their schools are in the top 10%.

In the 2024–2025 school year, being in the top 10% means a school's reading proficiency is about 11 or more percentage points higher than the model predicts given its context. This is a high bar. On average, schools in this group perform about 16 points above their predicted levels.

There are 45 districts with 4 or more elementary schools. There are 422 elementary schools in these districts. Thirty-three schools, or 7.8%, are in the top 10%. This means large districts, as a group, have fewer top-performing schools than would be expected if performance were evenly distributed. Twenty districts have at least one school in this high-performance, grade A category. Most do not. Thirteen districts have one grade A school, and seven districts have more than one. Anoka-Hennepin has four grade A schools, the highest number in any district. That is 16% of its elementary schools. Eden Prairie has three grade A elementary schools, which is half of its elementary schools. That is the highest percentage of grade A schools among large districts.

The goal of the School Progress Project is not to place every school in the top 10%, which is mathematically impossible. Rather, the goal is to help more schools outperform their predicted achievement given their context and their post-READ Act starting point.

Observation 3

A higher share of schools in small districts are in the top 10%. To explore this group, select "Districts with 1-3 elementary schools" in Overall View. Then choose a few familiar districts and see how many of their schools are in the top 10%.

There are 408 districts with 1 to 3 elementary schools. Together, they include 480 elementary schools. Eighteen districts have three schools, thirty-seven have two schools, and the remaining districts have a single elementary school.

Fifty-eight schools in small districts, or 12%, are in the top 10%, the high-performance, grade A category. In contrast, only 7.8% of schools in larger districts are in this category. Small districts are therefore somewhat more likely to produce schools that substantially outperform expectations.

### Observation 4

District size and poverty interact. Compare schools in small districts (1-3 elementary schools) with schools in larger districts (4+ elementary schools). As districts become wealthier, larger districts are more likely to produce top 20% schools. As poverty increases, smaller districts are more likely to produce top 20% schools.

This pattern can be seen directly in the plot. First, use the Overall View tool to toggle between small districts and districts with 4+ elementary schools. Select a few districts you care about-your own district, a neighboring district, an urban district, or a small town you know well. Each view includes a regression line. Notice that the lines have different slopes.

At higher levels of poverty, the predicted achievement line for small districts is higher. (This contrast is more visible in the Model Explorer page in the panel titled "Predicted Proficiency by Poverty and District Structure.") For example, at 80% FRPL, a school in a small district is predicted to have about 5 percentage points more students proficient in reading than a comparable school in a larger district. A five-point difference is substantial. Over multiple years, most schools see only small changes in proficiency, with gains and losses often offsetting each other.

Second, return to the "All schools" view. As poverty increases, notice two patterns. First, high-poverty schools are more common in small districts. Nearly two-thirds of schools serving 80%+ FRPL enrollments are in districts with 1-3 elementary schools. Second, high-performing schools at these poverty levels are overwhelmingly concentrated in small districts. There are 11 schools in the top 10% serving 80%+ FRPL enrollments; 10 of them are in small districts. There are 23 schools in the top 20%, 18 of which are in small districts. This concentration is not random. It reflects a consistent pattern in how small districts respond to higher levels of poverty.

These schools pull the small-district regression line upward. This leads to the next observation.

### Observation 5

Smallness does not automatically confer advantage. District size and poverty interact in ways that shift performance patterns. Larger districts tend to outperform smaller districts as wealth increases. Smaller districts tend to outperform larger districts as poverty increases. Smallness, therefore, can be understood as an unrealized opportunity rather than an inherent advantage.

If we can understand what allows some small-district schools to outperform expectations, those conditions may be transferable to schools in larger systems. The goal is not to make every district small, but to identify and replicate the practices that allow some schools to respond effectively to higher levels of poverty.

The advantages associated with size do not operate in the same way in reverse. Larger districts often have more resources and may benefit more directly from increases in community wealth-through referenda, parent organizations, and private contributions. Wealthier districts also tend to serve families with higher levels of formal education, more stable economic conditions, and greater access to early learning opportunities.

This raises an important question: what is it about larger systems that makes it more difficult to respond effectively as poverty increases? Understanding that constraint is central to improving outcomes at scale.

### Observation 6

There is a disconnection between institutions and achievement. In the Overall View tool, select "Highlight MESPA award schools." MESPA is the Minnesota Elementary School Principals' Association. The highlighted schools are led by principals who received MESPA awards in the 2024-2025 school year. Notice that these 24 schools do not cluster consistently at the top of the achievement distribution. Three are in the lowest 20% of achievement, six are in the top 20%, and the remaining fifteen are in the middle 60%.

This does not mean the awards are meaningless. It does indicate a disconnection between professional recognition and student achievement. The scatterplot suggests that institutional recognition, at least in this case, is not tightly connected to unusually strong reading outcomes.

MESPA also has policy priorities. Those priorities focus largely on the conditions under which principals work, including funding, local control, and mandates. In this analysis, there is no obvious sign that those priorities are closely tied to stronger literacy outcomes. That raises a larger question: to what extent are Minnesota's educational institutions explicitly organized around improving student achievement, especially reading achievement?

MESPA is only one example, and it appears here because the award data were available and quantifiable. But Minnesota did not slide below the national average in NAEP 4th grade literacy proficiency in 2022 because of MESPA. The broader concern reaches beyond one organization. If public institutions are serious about reversing academic decline, they should be able to show a clearer connection between what they celebrate, what they prioritize, and what students achieve.
